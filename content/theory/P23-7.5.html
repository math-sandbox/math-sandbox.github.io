7.5 Partial fractions and binomial expansions

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Pure Mathematics 2 and 3 Coursebook (Sue Pemberton, Julian Gilbey), Chapter 7, printed pp.179–181. Own wording and new numbers, following the coursebook method of splitting a rational expression into partial fractions and then expanding each partial fraction with the binomial expansion.

Key words: partial fractions, binomial expansion

Why split first

A rational expression such as \(\dfrac{3}{(1+x)(1-2x)}\) is hard to expand directly. Written in partial fractions, it becomes a sum of simple terms such as \(\dfrac{1}{1+x}\) and \(\dfrac{2}{1-2x}\), and each of these has a known binomial expansion.

The method

  1. Express the function in partial fractions.
  2. Write each partial fraction as a power of a bracket, for example \(\dfrac{2}{1-2x}=2(1-2x)^{-1}\) or \(\dfrac{5}{(3-x)^2}=\dfrac{5}{9}\left(1-\dfrac{x}{3}\right)^{-2}\).
  3. Expand each one up to the required power of \(x\), and add the terms with the same power.

Each expansion has its own range of validity. The expansion of the whole function is valid where all of them are valid, which is the narrowest of the ranges.

Worked example 1: Two linear factors

Coursebook, 7.5, partial fractions then expansion. New numbers.

Express \(\dfrac{5+x}{(1-x)(2+x)}\) in partial fractions, and hence expand it in ascending powers of \(x\) up to and including the term in \(x^2\).

  1. \(5+x=A(2+x)+B(1-x)\). Put \(x=1\): \(6=3A\), so \(A=2\). Put \(x=-2\): \(3=3B\), so \(B=1\). M1
  2. \(\dfrac{2}{1-x}+\dfrac{1}{2+x}\) A1
  3. \(\dfrac{2}{1-x}=2+2x+2x^2\) and \(\dfrac{1}{2+x}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(1+\dfrac{x}{2}\right)^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{2}-\dfrac{x}{4}+\dfrac{x^2}{8}\). M1
  4. \(\dfrac{5}{2}+\dfrac{7}{4}x+\dfrac{17}{8}x^2\) A1

Answer: \(\dfrac{2}{1-x}+\dfrac{1}{2+x}\); \(\dfrac{5}{2}+\dfrac{7}{4}x+\dfrac{17}{8}x^2\)

Worked example 2: The range of validity

Coursebook, 7.5, the combined range of validity. New numbers.

Given that \(\dfrac{3}{(1+x)(1-2x)}=\dfrac{1}{1+x}+\dfrac{2}{1-2x}\), expand the expression up to and including the term in \(x^2\), and state the set of values of \(x\) for which the expansion is valid.

  1. \(\dfrac{1}{1+x}=1-x+x^2\) and \(\dfrac{2}{1-2x}=2+4x+8x^2\). M1
  2. \(3+3x+9x^2\) A1
  3. The expansions are valid for \(\lvert x\rvert<1\) and \(\lvert x\rvert<\dfrac{1}{2}\), so both hold for \(\lvert x\rvert<\dfrac{1}{2}\). B1

Answer: \(3+3x+9x^2\), valid for \(\lvert x\rvert<\dfrac{1}{2}\)

Common mistakes

  • Expanding \(\dfrac{B}{2+x}\) as \(B(1+x)^{-1}\) without first taking out the factor \(\dfrac{1}{2}\).
  • For a repeated factor, forgetting that \(\dfrac{C}{(3-x)^2}\) needs the power \(-2\), with the factor \(\dfrac{1}{9}\) taken out.
  • Giving the widest range of validity instead of the narrowest one.

Quick check 1

Given that \(\mathrm{f}(x)=\dfrac{5+4x}{(1-x)(1+2x)}\) can be written as \(\dfrac{A}{1-x}+\dfrac{B}{1+2x}\), find the value of \(A\).

Worked solution
  1. \(5+4x=A(1+2x)+B(1-x)\). Put \(x=1\): \(9=3A\).

Answer: \(A=3\)

Quick check 2

Given that \(\mathrm{f}(x)=\dfrac{5+7x}{(1-x)(1+2x)}=\dfrac{4}{1-x}+\dfrac{1}{1+2x}\), find the coefficient of \(x\) in the expansion of \(\mathrm{f}(x)\) in ascending powers of \(x\).

Worked solution
  1. \(\dfrac{4}{1-x}=4+4x+\cdots\) and \(\dfrac{1}{1+2x}=1-2x+\cdots\)
  2. Coefficient of \(x\): \(4-2=2\)

Answer: \(2\)

Quick check 3

Given that \(\mathrm{f}(x)=\dfrac{6+3x}{(1-x)(1+2x)}=\dfrac{3}{1-x}+\dfrac{3}{1+2x}\), find the coefficient of \(x^2\) in the expansion of \(\mathrm{f}(x)\) in ascending powers of \(x\).

Worked solution
  1. \(\dfrac{3}{1-x}=3+3x+3x^2+\cdots\) and \(\dfrac{3}{1+2x}=3-6x+12x^2-\cdots\)
  2. Coefficient of \(x^2\): \(3+12=15\)

Answer: \(15\)
