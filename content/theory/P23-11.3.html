11.3 The complex plane

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Pure Mathematics 2 & 3 Coursebook (Sue Pemberton, series editor Julian Gilbey), Chapter 11 Complex numbers, printed pp.273–282. Own wording and new numbers, following the coursebook Argand diagram, modulus and argument, the modulus–argument form and the exponential form, with the rules for products and quotients.

Key words: Argand diagram, modulus of a complex number, argument, modulus-argument form, exponential form

The Argand diagram

The complex number \(x+\mathrm{i}y\) is shown as the point \((x, y)\) on an Argand diagram: the horizontal axis is the real axis and the vertical axis is the imaginary axis.

Modulus and argument

The modulus of a complex number \(z\) is \(|z|=r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), the distance of the point from the origin. The argument \(\arg z=\theta\) is the angle from the positive real axis to the line from \(O\) to the point, measured anticlockwise, with \(-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi\). Draw a sketch to see which quadrant the point is in before using \(\tan^{-1}\dfrac{y}{x}\).

Modulus-argument and exponential forms

Since \(x=r\cos\theta\) and \(y=r\sin\theta\), every non-zero complex number can be written in modulus-argument form \(z=r(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta)\), and in exponential form \(z=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\), using \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}=\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta\).

Products and quotients

\[|z_1z_2|=|z_1||z_2|,\quad \arg(z_1z_2)=\arg z_1+\arg z_2,\quad \left|\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right|=\frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|},\quad \arg\frac{z_1}{z_2}=\arg z_1-\arg z_2.\]

In exponential form: \(r_1\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}\times r_2\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\beta}=r_1r_2\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha+\beta)}\). After adding or subtracting, add or subtract \(2\pi\) to bring the argument into \(-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi\).

Worked example 1: Modulus and argument

Coursebook, 11.3, modulus and argument of a point in the second quadrant. New numbers.

Find the modulus and argument of \(z=-1+\sqrt3\,\mathrm{i}\).

  1. \(|z|=\sqrt{1+3}=2\) B1
  2. The point is in the second quadrant; \(\tan^{-1}\sqrt3=\dfrac{\pi}{3}\) M1
  3. \(\arg z=\pi-\dfrac{\pi}{3}=\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\) A1

Answer: \(|z|=2\), \(\arg z=\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\), so \(z=2\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2}{3}\pi\mathrm{i}}\)

Worked example 2: A quotient in exponential form

Coursebook, 11.3, dividing in exponential form. New numbers.

Given \(u=6\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{6}\pi\mathrm{i}}\) and \(w=2\mathrm{e}^{\frac{3}{4}\pi\mathrm{i}}\), find \(\dfrac{u}{w}\) in the form \(r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\), where \(-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi\).

  1. \(r=\dfrac{6}{2}=3\) B1
  2. \(\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{6}-\dfrac{3\pi}{4}=-\dfrac{7\pi}{12}\) M1 A1

Answer: \(3\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{7}{12}\pi\mathrm{i}}\)

Common mistakes

  • Using \(\tan^{-1}\dfrac{y}{x}\) without checking the quadrant.
  • Giving an argument outside \(-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi\).
  • Adding the moduli instead of multiplying them for a product.

9709/32/M/J/23 Q5, official QP and official MS.

The complex number \(2+y\mathrm{i}\) is denoted by \(a\), where \(y\) is a real number and \(y<0\). It is given that \(\mathrm{f}(a)=a^3-a^2-2a\).

(a) Find a simplified expression for \(\mathrm{f}(a)\) in terms of \(y\). [3]

(b) Given that \(\mathrm{Re}\bigl(\mathrm{f}(a)\bigr)=-20\), find \(\arg a\). [3]

Mark scheme
  1. (a) \(a^2=4+4y\mathrm{i}-y^2\), \(a^3=8+12y\mathrm{i}-6y^2-y^3\mathrm{i}\) M1 Official MS: M1, substitute 2 + yi in a³ − a² − 2a and attempt expansions of a² and a³; must see working.
  2. (a) Use \(\mathrm{i}^2=-1\) M1 Official MS: M1, seen at least once (e.g. in squaring).
  3. (a) \(\mathrm{f}(a)=-5y^2+(6y-y^3)\mathrm{i}\) A1 Official MS: A1, obtain final answer −5y² + (6y − y³)i, or simplified equivalent; do not ISW.
  4. (b) \(-5y^2=-20\), so \(y^2=4\) M1 Official MS: M1, equate their −5y² to −20 and solve for y.
  5. (b) \(y=-2\) A1 Official MS: A1, from correct work (must have rejected y positive).
  6. (b) \(a=2-2\mathrm{i}\), so \(\arg a=-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\) A1 Official MS: A1, obtain final answer arg a = −π/4 OE; accept −0.785, 5.50; accept degrees.

The complex numbers \(u\) and \(w\) are defined by \(u=2\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{4}\pi\mathrm{i}}\) and \(w=3\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{3}\pi\mathrm{i}}\).

(a) Find \(\dfrac{u^2}{w}\), giving your answer in the form \(r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\), where \(r>0\) and \(-\pi<\theta\le\pi\). Give the exact values of \(r\) and \(\theta\). [3]

(b) State the least positive integer \(n\) such that both \(\mathrm{Im}\,w^n=0\) and \(\mathrm{Re}\,w^n>0\). [1]

Mark scheme
  1. (a) \(u^2=4\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}\pi\mathrm{i}}\) B1 Official MS: B1, state or imply u² = 4e^(πi/2).
  2. (a) Modulus \(r=\dfrac{4}{3}\) B1 Official MS: B1 + B1 for the answer (4/3)e^(πi/6), for the modulus and the argument; this is the modulus mark.
  3. (a) Argument \(\theta=\dfrac{1}{2}\pi-\dfrac{1}{3}\pi=\dfrac{1}{6}\pi\), so \(\dfrac{u^2}{w}=\dfrac{4}{3}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{6}\pi\mathrm{i}}\) B1 Official MS: B1 + B1, this is the argument mark.
  4. (b) \(n=6\) B1 Official MS: B1, state n = 6.
