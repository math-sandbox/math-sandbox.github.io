8.3 Integration of \(\dfrac{k\,\mathrm{f}'(x)}{\mathrm{f}(x)}\)

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Pure Mathematics 2 & 3 Coursebook (Sue Pemberton, series editor Julian Gilbey), Chapter 8 Further calculus, printed pp.189–190. Own wording and new numbers, following the coursebook rule for integrals where the numerator is a multiple of the derivative of the denominator.

Key words: natural logarithm, chain rule

The rule

By the chain rule, \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\ln\bigl(\mathrm{f}(x)\bigr)=\dfrac{\mathrm{f}'(x)}{\mathrm{f}(x)}\). Reversing this gives

\[\int\frac{k\,\mathrm{f}'(x)}{\mathrm{f}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x=k\ln\bigl\lvert\mathrm{f}(x)\bigr\rvert+c.\]

So when the numerator is a multiple of the derivative of the denominator, the answer is a multiple of the natural logarithm of the denominator. Check by differentiating the denominator and comparing it with the numerator, then adjust the constant.

Trigonometric examples: \(\displaystyle\int\tan x\,\mathrm{d}x=\int\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\,\mathrm{d}x=-\ln\lvert\cos x\rvert+c\), and \(\displaystyle\int\frac{\cos x}{2+\sin x}\,\mathrm{d}x=\ln(2+\sin x)+c\).

Worked example 1: Spotting the derivative

Coursebook, 8.3, recognising f'(x) over f(x). New numbers.

Find \(\displaystyle\int\frac{4x+6}{x^2+3x+1}\,\mathrm{d}x\).

  1. The derivative of \(x^2+3x+1\) is \(2x+3\), and \(4x+6=2(2x+3)\). M1
  2. \(2\ln\lvert x^2+3x+1\rvert+c\) A1

Answer: \(2\ln\lvert x^2+3x+1\rvert+c\)

Worked example 2: A trigonometric integral

Coursebook, 8.3, logarithmic integrals with trigonometric functions. New numbers.

Find the exact value of \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/3}\tan x\,\mathrm{d}x\).

  1. \(\tan x=\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\) and the derivative of \(\cos x\) is \(-\sin x\). M1
  2. \(\displaystyle\int\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\,\mathrm{d}x=-\ln(\cos x)\) A1
  3. \(-\ln\frac{1}{2}+\ln1=\ln2\) A1

Answer: \(\ln2\)

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the constant when the numerator is only a multiple of the derivative, for example \(\displaystyle\int\frac{x}{x^2+1}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\ln(x^2+1)+c\).
  • Using the rule when the numerator is not a multiple of the derivative, such as \(\dfrac{1}{x^2+1}\), which gives an inverse tangent instead.
  • Losing the minus sign for \(\tan x\): the derivative of \(\cos x\) is \(-\sin x\).

  5. (ii) The integral is of the form \(a\ln(1+\cos x)\) M1 Official MS: M1*, a ln(1 + cos x). Substitution u = 1 + cos x allows M1 A1 for −ln u.
  6. (ii) \(-\ln(1+\cos x)\) A1 Official MS: A1.
