5.3 Integration of sin(ax + b), cos(ax + b) and sec²(ax + b)

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Pure Mathematics 2 & 3 Coursebook (Sue Pemberton, Julianne Hughes), Chapter 5 Integration, pp.118–120 (Key point 5.4, Worked examples 5.6–5.7, Exercise 5C). Own wording and new numbers.

Key words: reverse differentiation, reverse chain rule, indefinite integral, definite integral, constant of integration

Reversing the trigonometric derivatives

In Section 4.5 the derivatives of sine, cosine and tangent were found. Integration is reverse differentiation, so those results can be turned around. In particular, because the derivative of \(\sin x\) is \(\cos x\), the indefinite integral of \(\cos x\) is \(\sin x\) plus a constant of integration \(c\). The same idea gives the integrals of \(\sin x\) and \(\sec^2 x\). These formulae hold only when the angle is in radians.

$$\int\cos x\,\mathrm{d}x=\sin x+c,\qquad\int\sin x\,\mathrm{d}x=-\cos x+c,\qquad\int\sec^2 x\,\mathrm{d}x=\tan x+c.$$

The minus sign in \(\int\sin x\,\mathrm{d}x=-\cos x+c\) is easy to lose: the derivative of \(\cos x\) is \(-\sin x\), so reversing it introduces the minus.

The reverse chain rule

For a linear argument \(ax+b\) with \(a\neq0\), the chain rule multiplies by \(a\). Reversing it, we divide by \(a\):

$$\int\cos(ax+b)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{a}\sin(ax+b)+c,\qquad\int\sin(ax+b)\,\mathrm{d}x=-\frac{1}{a}\cos(ax+b)+c,$$ $$\int\sec^2(ax+b)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{a}\tan(ax+b)+c\qquad(a\neq0).$$

This is the reverse chain rule: keep the trigonometric function that is the reverse of the one you are integrating, and divide by the coefficient of \(x\). A constant multiplier stays in front: \(\int k\sin(ax+b)\,\mathrm{d}x=-\dfrac{k}{a}\cos(ax+b)+c\).

If \(a=0\) the integrand is a constant and these formulae do not apply. For a definite integral, substitute the limits after finding the antiderivative; there is then no \(+c\).

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying by \(a\) instead of dividing: \(\int\cos 3x\,\mathrm{d}x\neq3\sin 3x+c\).
  • Losing the minus sign when integrating sine.
  • Leaving out \(+c\) from an indefinite integral.
  • Using a calculator in degree mode. In calculus, angles are in radians unless a question says otherwise.

Worked example 1: Using the reverse chain rule

Book: Worked example 5.6, p.119; Exercise 5C Q1, p.120

Find (a) \(\int\sin 4x\,\mathrm{d}x\) (b) \(\int\cos(3x-1)\,\mathrm{d}x\) (c) \(\int 6\sec^2\dfrac{x}{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\).

  1. (a) \(a=4\): \(-\dfrac{1}{4}\cos 4x+c\) B1
  2. (b) \(a=3\): \(\dfrac{1}{3}\sin(3x-1)+c\) B1
  3. (c) \(a=\dfrac12\): \(6\times\dfrac{1}{1/2}\tan\dfrac{x}{2}+c=12\tan\dfrac{x}{2}+c\) B1

Answer: (a) \(-\dfrac14\cos 4x+c\) (b) \(\dfrac13\sin(3x-1)+c\) (c) \(12\tan\dfrac{x}{2}+c\)

Worked example 2: A definite integral

Book: Worked example 5.7, p.119; Exercise 5C Q2, p.120

Find the exact value of \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/6}(4-6\sin 2x)\,\mathrm{d}x\).

  1. Antiderivative: \(4x-6\times\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\cos 2x\right)=4x+3\cos 2x\) M1 A1
  2. \(\bigl[4x+3\cos 2x\bigr]_0^{\pi/6}=\left(4\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{6}+3\cos\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right)-\bigl(0+3\cos 0\bigr)\) M1
  3. \(=\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}+3\cdot\dfrac12\right)-(3\cdot1)=\dfrac{2\pi}{3}+\dfrac32-3=\dfrac{2\pi}{3}-\dfrac32\) A1

Answer: \(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}-\dfrac32\)

Worked example 3: Finding the constant of integration

Book: Exercise 5C Q4, p.120 (constant of integration after a trigonometric integral)

A curve has gradient \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}=4\cos 2x\) and passes through the point \(\left(\dfrac{\pi}{6},\,1\right)\). Find the equation of the curve.

  1. \(y=4\times\dfrac{1}{2}\sin 2x+c=2\sin 2x+c\) M1 A1
  2. At \(x=\dfrac{\pi}{6}\), \(y=1\): \(2\sin\dfrac{\pi}{3}+c=1\) M1
  3. \(2\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}+c=1\), so \(c=1-\sqrt{3}\) A1

Answer: \(y=2\sin 2x+1-\sqrt{3}\)

Quick checks

Quick check 1

Find \(\int 6\sin 2x\,\mathrm{d}x\).

Worked solution
  1. \(a=2\): \(6\times\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\cos 2x\right)+c=-3\cos 2x+c\)

Answer: \(-3\cos 2x+c\)

Quick check 2

Find the exact value of \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}6\cos 3x\,\mathrm{d}x\).

Worked solution
  1. \(\bigl[6\times\dfrac{1}{3}\sin 3x\bigr]_0^{\pi/2}=\bigl[2\sin 3x\bigr]_0^{\pi/2}\)
  2. \(=2\sin\dfrac{3\pi}{2}-2\sin 0=2(-1)-0=-2\)

Answer: \(-2\)

Quick check 3

Find the exact value of \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/8}4\sec^2 2x\,\mathrm{d}x\).

Worked solution
  1. \(\bigl[4\times\dfrac{1}{2}\tan 2x\bigr]_0^{\pi/8}=\bigl[2\tan 2x\bigr]_0^{\pi/8}\)
  2. \(=2\tan\dfrac{\pi}{4}-2\tan 0=2\cdot1-0=2\)

Answer: \(2\)

Quick check 4

A curve has gradient \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}=6\cos 2x\) and passes through the point \((0,\,1)\). Find the equation of the curve.

Worked solution
  1. \(y=6\times\dfrac{1}{2}\sin 2x+c=3\sin 2x+c\)
  2. At \(x=0\), \(y=1\): \(3\sin 0+c=1\), so \(c=1\)

Answer: \(y=3\sin 2x+1\)

Marks: M = method, A = accurate answer (depends on M), B = independent mark. Marks on worked examples are illustrative, not official. Give exact answers unless the question asks for a decimal. In calculus, angles are in radians unless a question says otherwise. Always include \(+c\) in an indefinite integral, and check by differentiating.
