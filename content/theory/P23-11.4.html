11.4 Solving equations

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Pure Mathematics 2 & 3 Coursebook (Sue Pemberton, series editor Julian Gilbey), Chapter 11 Complex numbers, printed pp.283–287. Own wording and new numbers, following the coursebook method for square roots of a complex number, the conjugate-pair property of real polynomials and solving quadratic and cubic equations.

Key words: complex conjugate, conjugate pair, square root, quadratic equation

Square roots of a complex number

To find the square roots of \(a+b\mathrm{i}\), let \((x+\mathrm{i}y)^2=a+b\mathrm{i}\). Expanding gives \(x^2-y^2+2xy\mathrm{i}\), so

\[x^2-y^2=a,\qquad 2xy=b.\]

Substitute \(y=\dfrac{b}{2x}\) into the first equation to get a quadratic equation in \(x^2\). Only the positive value of \(x^2\) is allowed because \(x\) is real. The two square roots are \(\pm(x+\mathrm{i}y)\).

Conjugate pairs

If a polynomial equation has real coefficients and \(z\) is a root, then its complex conjugate \(z^*\) is also a root: complex roots occur in conjugate pairs. The pair \(a\pm b\mathrm{i}\) gives the real quadratic factor

\[(x-(a+b\mathrm{i}))(x-(a-b\mathrm{i}))=x^2-2ax+(a^2+b^2).\]

For a cubic with one known complex root, write down the conjugate, form this quadratic factor, and divide (or compare coefficients) to find the real root.

Quadratics with complex coefficients

The quadratic formula still works when the coefficients are complex. Simplify the discriminant using \(\mathrm{i}^2=-1\), and divide by a complex number by multiplying by its conjugate.

Worked example 1: Square roots

Coursebook, 11.4, finding the square roots of a complex number. New numbers.

Find the square roots of \(-15+8\mathrm{i}\).

  1. \(x^2-y^2=-15\), \(2xy=8\) M1 A1
  2. \(y=\dfrac{4}{x}\): \(x^4+15x^2-16=0\), \((x^2+16)(x^2-1)=0\) M1
  3. \(x^2=1\), \(x=\pm1\), \(y=\pm4\) A1

Answer: \(\pm(1+4\mathrm{i})\)

Worked example 2: A cubic

Coursebook, 11.4, a cubic equation with a known complex root. New numbers.

Given that \(1+2\mathrm{i}\) is a root of \(x^3-x^2+3x+5=0\), find the other roots.

  1. \(1-2\mathrm{i}\) is also a root B1
  2. Quadratic factor \(x^2-2x+5\) M1
  3. \(x^3-x^2+3x+5=(x^2-2x+5)(x+1)\) M1
  4. Other roots \(1-2\mathrm{i}\) and \(-1\) A1

Answer: \(1-2\mathrm{i}\) and \(-1\)

Common mistakes

  • Keeping the negative value of \(x^2\) when finding square roots: \(x\) must be real.
  • Giving only one square root instead of the pair \(\pm(x+\mathrm{i}y)\).
  • Using the conjugate-pair property when the coefficients are not all real.

Quick check 1

Find the square roots of \(5+12\mathrm{i}\).

Worked solution
  1. \(x^2-y^2=5\), \(xy=6\)
  2. \(x^4-5x^2-36=0\), \(x^2=9\)

Answer: \(\pm(3+2\mathrm{i})\)

Quick check 2

\(x^2+px+q=0\), with \(p\) and \(q\) real, has a root \(2+3\mathrm{i}\). Find \(q\).

Worked solution
  1. Other root \(2-3\mathrm{i}\)
  2. \(q=4+9\)

Answer: \(13\)

Quick check 3

Given that \(-1+2\mathrm{i}\) is a root of \(x^3+5x^2+11x+15=0\), find the real root.

Worked solution
  1. Quadratic factor \(x^2+2x+5\)
  2. \(15=5\times3\), factor \(x+3\)

Answer: \(-3\)
