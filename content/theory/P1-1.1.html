1.1 Solving quadratic equations by factorisation

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Pure Mathematics 1 Coursebook (Sue Pemberton), Chapter 1 Quadratics, printed pp.3–6 (Worked examples 1.1–1.4 and the factorisation method). Printed pages were read from P1.pdf (PDF pages 15–18). Own wording and new numbers.

Key words: factorisation

Putting the equation into standard form

A quadratic equation can be written in the form \(ax^2+bx+c=0\), where \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) are constants and \(a\neq 0\). One algebraic method of solving it is factorisation: rewrite the left-hand side as two linear factors multiplied together and then use the fact that if \(pq=0\), then \(p=0\) or \(q=0\).

If there is a common numerical factor, divide it out first. If the equation is not already in the form \(ax^2+bx+c=0\), rearrange it before you factorise. When you have two factors, set each factor equal to zero and solve. Each solution is a root of the original equation. You can check a root by substituting it back into the original equation.

Worked example 1: Two linear factors

Book: Worked example 1.1, printed p.3. New coefficients.

Solve \(8x^2-14x+3=0\) by factorisation.

  1. The left-hand side factorises as \((4x-1)(2x-3)\) M1
  2. So \(4x-1=0\) or \(2x-3=0\), giving \(x=\dfrac{1}{4}\) or \(x=\dfrac{3}{2}\) A1 A1

Answer: \(x=\dfrac{1}{4}\) or \(x=\dfrac{3}{2}\)

Worked example 2: Rearrange, then factorise

Book: Worked example 1.1 part (a) style (rearrange first), printed p.3. New equation.

Solve \(10x^2+x=3\) by factorisation.

  1. Rearrange: \(10x^2+x-3=0\) M1
  2. Factorise: \((5x+3)(2x-1)=0\) M1
  3. So \(5x+3=0\) or \(2x-1=0\), giving \(x=-\dfrac{3}{5}\) or \(x=\dfrac{1}{2}\) A1 A1

Answer: \(x=-\dfrac{3}{5}\) or \(x=\dfrac{1}{2}\)

Common mistakes

  • Dividing both sides of an equation by an expression that could be zero, and so losing a root. (Explore 1.1 in the book is a warning of this kind.)
  • Forgetting to rearrange into the form \(ax^2+bx+c=0\) before factorising.
  • Writing the constant term or the middle term with the wrong sign when choosing the factors.

Quick checks

Quick check 1

Solve \(x^2-5x-14=0\) by factorisation. Write down the larger root.

Worked solution
  1. \(x^2-5x-14=(x-7)(x+2)\) M1
  2. The roots are \(x=7\) and \(x=-2\). The larger root is \(7\) A1

Answer: \(7\)

Quick check 2

Solve \(3x^2-5x-2=0\) by factorisation. Write down the positive root.

Worked solution
  1. \(3x^2-5x-2=(3x+1)(x-2)\) M1
  2. The roots are \(x=-\dfrac{1}{3}\) and \(x=2\). The positive root is \(2\) A1

Answer: \(2\)

Quick check 3

Solve \(x^2+x-12=0\) by factorisation. Write down the positive root.

Worked solution
  1. \(x^2+x-12=(x+4)(x-3)\) M1
  2. The roots are \(x=-4\) and \(x=3\). The positive root is \(3\) A1

Answer: \(3\)

Marks: M = method, A = accurate answer (depends on M), B = independent mark. Rearrange into \(ax^2+bx+c=0\) first, factorise, and then set each factor equal to zero. Do not divide by an expression that might be zero.
