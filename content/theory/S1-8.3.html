8.3 Modelling with the normal distribution

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Probability & Statistics 1 Coursebook (Dean Chalmers), Chapter 8 The normal distribution, printed pp.205–207. Own wording and new numbers, following the coursebook use of a normal model to estimate numbers in a sample, to combine a normal probability with a binomial probability, and to find an unknown parameter from observed data.

Key words: normal distribution, standardise, expected number, relative frequency

Using a normal model

Many real quantities, such as lengths, masses and times, are well modelled by a normal distribution. Once the model \(X\sim\mathrm{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) is chosen, every probability is found by the method of 8.2: standardise with \(z=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\) and read \(\Phi(z)\) from the table. A sketch of the curve with the required area shaded shows whether you need \(\Phi(z)\), \(1-\Phi(z)\) or a difference of two values.

Expected numbers

A probability from the model is a long-run relative frequency. In a sample of \(n\) items the expected number with a property of probability \(p\) is \(np\). The model only gives an estimate, so a count of items is usually rounded to a whole number.

For values within \(d\) of the mean, the symmetry of the curve gives

\[P(\mu-d\lt X\lt \mu+d)=P(-z\lt Z\lt z)=2\Phi(z)-1,\qquad z=\dfrac{d}{\sigma}.\]

Combining with the binomial distribution

If each of several independent items has the same probability \(p\) of a property, found from the normal model, the number of items with that property has the binomial distribution \(\mathrm{B}(m, p)\). For example, the probability that exactly one of \(m\) items has the property is \(m\,p\,(1-p)^{m-1}\).

Finding an unknown parameter from data

If \(c\) out of \(N\) observed values are greater than \(x\), use \(\dfrac{c}{N}\) as an estimate of \(P(X\gt x)\). Read the \(z\)-value from the table and solve \(\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}=z\) for the unknown \(\mu\) or \(\sigma\).

Worked example 1: An expected number

Coursebook, 8.3, estimating a number from a normal model. New numbers.

The masses of apples are normally distributed with mean 150 g and standard deviation 12 g. Estimate how many of a batch of 800 apples have a mass less than 165 g.

  1. \(z=\dfrac{165-150}{12}=1.25\) M1
  2. \(P(X\lt 165)=\Phi(1.25)=0.8944\) A1
  3. Expected number \(=800\times0.8944=715.52\), so about 716 apples A1

Answer: about 716 apples

Worked example 2: Normal and binomial together

Coursebook, 8.3, a normal probability used in a binomial probability. New numbers.

The volume of oil in a can is normally distributed with mean 502 ml and standard deviation 2.5 ml. Find the probability that exactly 2 of 4 randomly chosen cans contain less than 500 ml.

  1. \(z=\dfrac{500-502}{2.5}=-0.8\), so \(P(X\lt 500)=1-\Phi(0.8)=1-0.7881=0.2119\) M1
  2. \(P(\text{exactly 2})=\binom{4}{2}(0.2119)^2(0.7881)^2\) M1
  3. \(=6\times0.044902\times0.621102=0.167\) A1

Answer: \(0.167\) (3 s.f.)

Common mistakes

  • Giving a probability when the question asks for a number of items, or a decimal such as 715.52 when a whole number is expected.
  • Using \(\Phi(z)\) instead of \(2\Phi(z)-1\) for 'within \(d\) of the mean'.
  • Using the number of items \(c\) instead of the relative frequency \(\dfrac{c}{N}\) when finding \(z\).

A company produces a particular type of metal rod. The lengths of these rods are normally distributed with mean 25.2 cm and standard deviation 0.4 cm. A random sample of 500 of these rods is chosen.

How many rods in this sample would you expect to have a length that is within 0.5 cm of the mean length? [5]

Past paper

9709/52/M/J/21 Q2, official QP and official MS.

The weights of bags of sugar are normally distributed with mean 1.04 kg and standard deviation \(\sigma\) kg. In a random sample of 2000 bags of sugar, 72 weighed more than 1.10 kg.

Find the value of \(\sigma\). [4]

Mark scheme
  1. \(P(X>1.1)=\dfrac{72}{2000}=0.036\), so \(z=1.798\) B1 Official MS: B1, 1.79 < z ⩽ 1.80 (or −1.80 ⩽ z < −1.79) seen.
  2. \(\dfrac{1.1-1.04}{\sigma}=1.798\) B1 Official MS: B1, 1.1 and 1.04 substituted in ± standardisation formula; allow continuity correction, not σ² or √σ.
  3. \(\sigma=\dfrac{0.06}{1.798}\) M1 Official MS: M1, equate their ± standardisation formula to a z-value and solve for the appropriate area leading to final answer (expect σ < 0.5).
  4. \(\sigma=0.0334\) A1 Official MS: A1, 0.03335 ⩽ σ ⩽ 0.0334, at least 3 s.f.

Quick check 1

\(X\sim\mathrm{N}(25, 4^2)\). Given that \(\Phi(1)=0.8413\), find the expected number of values within 4 of the mean in a random sample of 500 values, correct to 3 significant figures.

Worked solution
  1. \(z=\dfrac{4}{4}=1\)
  2. \(2\Phi(1)-1=0.6826\)
  3. \(500\times0.6826=341.3\)

Answer: \(341\) (3 s.f.)

Quick check 2

\(X\sim\mathrm{N}(50, 5^2)\). Given that \(\Phi(1)=0.8413\), find the probability that exactly one of 3 independent values of \(X\) is greater than 55.

Worked solution
  1. \(P(X\gt 55)=1-0.8413=0.1587\)
  2. \(3\times0.1587\times0.8413^2\)

Answer: \(0.337\) (3 s.f.)

Quick check 3

The values of a variable are normally distributed with mean 40 and standard deviation \(\sigma\). In a sample of 400 values, 40 were greater than 46. Given that \(\Phi(1.282)=0.9\), estimate \(\sigma\).

Worked solution
  1. \(P(X\gt 46)=\dfrac{40}{400}=0.1\), so \(z=1.282\)
  2. \(\dfrac{46-40}{\sigma}=1.282\)

Answer: \(\sigma=4.68\) (3 s.f.)
