1.2 Completing the square

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Pure Mathematics 1 Coursebook (Sue Pemberton), Chapter 1 Quadratics, printed pp.6–9 (Key point 1.1 and Worked examples 1.5–1.7). Printed pages were read from P1.pdf (PDF pages 18–21). Own wording and new numbers.

Key words: completing the square

Rewriting a quadratic with one occurrence of the variable

Another method for solving quadratic equations is completing the square. Completing the square rewrites a quadratic expression so that the variable appears only inside a squared bracket. Expanding \((x+d)^2\) and \((x-d)^2\) and rearranging gives the identities in Key point 1.1:

\[ x^2+2dx=(x+d)^2-d^2,\qquad x^2-2dx=(x-d)^2-d^2. \]

For a monic expression \(x^2+bx\), take half of \(b\) as the number inside the bracket, and subtract its square. If the quadratic has a leading coefficient other than 1, first take that coefficient out of the \(x^2\) and \(x\) terms, complete the square for the monic part, and then expand the factor through the constant term as well.

Worked example 1: Completing the square with a leading coefficient

Book: Worked example 1.5 and the \(2x^2-12x+5\) illustration, printed pp.7–8. New coefficients.

Express \(2x^2-8x+7\) in the form \(p(x-q)^2+r\).

  1. Take out the factor 2 from the first two terms: \(2x^2-8x+7=2(x^2-4x)+7\) M1
  2. Complete the square: \(x^2-4x=(x-2)^2-4\), so the expression is \(2\bigl((x-2)^2-4\bigr)+7=2(x-2)^2-8+7\) M1
  3. Hence \(2x^2-8x+7=2(x-2)^2-1\) A1

Answer: \(2(x-2)^2-1\)

Worked example 2: Solving by completing the square

Book: Worked examples 1.6–1.7 style (solve after completing the square), printed pp.8–9. New equation.

Solve \(x^2-6x-2=0\) by completing the square. Leave your answers in exact form.

  1. \(x^2-6x-2=(x-3)^2-9-2=(x-3)^2-11\) M1
  2. \((x-3)^2=11\), so \(x-3=\pm\sqrt{11}\) M1
  3. Hence \(x=3+\sqrt{11}\) or \(x=3-\sqrt{11}\) A1 A1

Answer: \(x=3\pm\sqrt{11}\)

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to multiply the \(-d^2\) term by the leading coefficient when that coefficient is not 1.
  • Using \(b\) instead of \(\dfrac{b}{2}\) inside the bracket.
  • Dropping the \(\pm\) when taking the square-root, and so finding only one root.

Quick checks

Quick check 1

The expression \(x^2+14x\) can be written as \((x+7)^2+k\). Find the value of \(k\).

Worked solution
  1. \(x^2+14x=(x+7)^2-49\), so \(k=-49\) A1

Answer: \(-49\)

Quick check 2

The expression \(2x^2-16x+7\) can be written as \(2(x-4)^2+k\). Find the value of \(k\).

Worked solution
  1. \(2x^2-16x+7=2(x^2-8x)+7=2\bigl((x-4)^2-16\bigr)+7=2(x-4)^2-32+7\), so \(k=-25\) M1 A1

Answer: \(-25\)

Quick check 3

Solve \(x^2-8x-9=0\) by completing the square. Write down the positive root.

Worked solution
  1. \(x^2-8x-9=(x-4)^2-16-9=(x-4)^2-25\) M1
  2. \((x-4)^2=25\), so \(x-4=\pm 5\) and \(x=9\) or \(x=-1\). The positive root is \(9\) A1

Answer: \(9\)

Marks: M = method, A = accurate answer (depends on M), B = independent mark. Halve the coefficient of \(x\) to complete the square, and remember to multiply \(-d^2\) by the leading coefficient when that coefficient is not 1.
