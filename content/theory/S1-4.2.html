4.2 Mutually exclusive events and the addition law

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Probability & Statistics 1 Coursebook (Dean Chalmers), Chapter 4 Probability, pp.94–98 (Key points 4.5–4.7, Worked examples 4.2–4.4, Exercise 4B). Own wording and new numbers.

Key words: mutually exclusive, addition law, Venn diagram, union, intersection, empty set, neither

Mutually exclusive events

Two events are mutually exclusive if they have no favourable outcomes in common, so they cannot both happen: \(\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)=0\). When one ordinary die is rolled, “even” \(=\{2,\ 4,\ 6\}\) and “a factor of 5” \(=\{1,\ 5\}\) are mutually exclusive, but “odd” \(=\{1,\ 3,\ 5\}\) and “a factor of 5” are not, because 1 and 5 are in both.

For mutually exclusive events we can add the probabilities. This is the addition law:

$$\mathrm{P}(A\text{ or }B)=\mathrm{P}(A)+\mathrm{P}(B),\qquad \mathrm{P}(A\text{ or }B\text{ or }C\text{ or }\ldots)=\mathrm{P}(A)+\mathrm{P}(B)+\mathrm{P}(C)+\ldots$$

Venn diagrams and set notation

A Venn diagram shows events as circles inside a rectangle ℰ, the set of all outcomes. Each region can hold outcomes, numbers of outcomes or probabilities. \(n(A)\) is the number of outcomes favourable to \(A\).

NotationMeaning

The union \(A\cup B\) means “\(A\) or \(B\)”, and in probability “or” always includes “both”. The intersection \(A\cap B\) means “both”. \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive exactly when their intersection is the empty set: \(A\cap B=\varnothing\).

The general addition law

If \(A\) and \(B\) overlap, adding \(n(A)\) and \(n(B)\) counts the outcomes in \(A\cap B\) twice, so we subtract them once:

$$n(A\cup B)=n(A)+n(B)-n(A\cap B),\qquad \mathrm{P}(A\cup B)=\mathrm{P}(A)+\mathrm{P}(B)-\mathrm{P}(A\cap B).$$

This works for any two events. For mutually exclusive events \(\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)=0\), and it becomes the simple addition law. The probability of neither event is \(1-\mathrm{P}(A\cup B)\).

Worked example 1: Which events are mutually exclusive?

Book: Worked example 4.2, pp.95–96; Exercise 4B Q1, p.98

One whole number is chosen at random from 1 to 12. \(A\): a multiple of 3. \(B\): a factor of 20. \(C\): an odd number. Show that \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive, and find \(\mathrm{P}(A\cup B)\), \(\mathrm{P}(A\cup C)\) and \(\mathrm{P}(B\cup C)\).

  1. \(A=\{3,\ 6,\ 9,\ 12\}\), \(B=\{1,\ 2,\ 4,\ 5,\ 10\}\), \(C=\{1,\ 3,\ 5,\ 7,\ 9,\ 11\}\) B1
  2. \(A\cap B=\varnothing\), so \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive B1
  3. \(\mathrm{P}(A\cup B)=\dfrac{4}{12}+\dfrac{5}{12}=\dfrac{9}{12}=\dfrac34\) B1
  4. \(A\cap C=\{3,\ 9\}\): \(\mathrm{P}(A\cup C)=\dfrac{4}{12}+\dfrac{6}{12}-\dfrac{2}{12}\) M1
  5. \(=\dfrac{8}{12}=\dfrac23\) A1
  6. \(B\cap C=\{1,\ 5\}\): \(\mathrm{P}(B\cup C)=\dfrac{5}{12}+\dfrac{6}{12}-\dfrac{2}{12}=\dfrac{9}{12}=\dfrac34\) A1

Answer: \(\mathrm{P}(A\cup B)=\frac34\), \(\mathrm{P}(A\cup C)=\frac23\), \(\mathrm{P}(B\cup C)=\frac34\)

Quick checks

Quick check 1

Events \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive, with \(\mathrm{P}(A)=0.42\) and \(\mathrm{P}(B)=0.35\). (a) Find \(\mathrm{P}(A\cup B)\). (b) Find the probability that neither \(A\) nor \(B\) occurs.

Worked solution
  1. (a) \(0.42+0.35=0.77\)
  2. (b) \(1-0.77=0.23\)

Answer: (a) 0.77 (b) 0.23

Quick check 2

Events \(A\) and \(B\) are such that \(\mathrm{P}(A)=0.6\), \(\mathrm{P}(B)=0.5\) and \(\mathrm{P}(A\cup B)=0.8\). Find \(\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)\).

Worked solution
  1. \(\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)=\mathrm{P}(A)+\mathrm{P}(B)-\mathrm{P}(A\cup B)=0.6+0.5-0.8\)

Answer: 0.3

Quick check 3

A whole number is chosen at random from 1 to 6. Find the probability that it is a multiple of 2 or a multiple of 3.

Worked solution
  1. Multiples of 2: 3; multiples of 3: 2; multiples of both (that is, of 6): 1
  2. \(n(A\cup B)=3+2-1=4\), so the probability is \(\dfrac46=\dfrac23\)

Answer: \(\frac23\approx0.667\)

Quick check 4

In a group of 30 students, 12 sing in the choir, 15 play in the band and 7 do neither. (a) Find the number of students who do both. (b) One student is chosen at random. Find the probability that the student does exactly one of these activities.

Worked solution
  1. (a) \(12+15+7-30=4\)
  2. (b) Choir only \(12-4=8\), band only \(15-4=11\): \(8+11=19\)
  3. \(\dfrac{19}{30}\approx0.633\)

Answer: (a) 4 (b) \(\frac{19}{30}\approx0.633\)

Marks: M = method, A = accurate answer (depends on M), B = independent mark. Marks on worked examples are illustrative, not official. In probability, “or” includes “both”; never count the overlap twice.
