5.4 Problem solving with permutations and combinations

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Probability & Statistics 1 Coursebook (Dean Chalmers), Chapter 5 Permutations and combinations, printed pp.138–142. Own wording and new numbers, following the coursebook use of equally likely permutations or combinations to find probabilities.

Key words: permutation, combination

Probabilities from counting

When all the possible arrangements, or all the possible selections, are equally likely, a probability can be found by counting:

\[\mathrm{P}(\text{event})=\dfrac{\text{number of favourable permutations}}{\text{number of possible permutations}}\quad\text{or}\quad\mathrm{P}(\text{event})=\dfrac{\text{number of favourable combinations}}{\text{number of possible combinations}}.\]

Use permutations when the order matters (people in a line, letters in a word) and combinations when it does not (a team, a hand of cards, letters taken from a bag). The numerator and the denominator must be counted in the same way.

Choosing a method

  • Many questions combine both ideas: choose the members of a group with combinations, then arrange them with permutations.
  • For “at least one”, the complement is often quicker: \(\mathrm{P}(\text{at least one})=1-\mathrm{P}(\text{none})\).
  • For several conditions together, list the scenarios that satisfy all of them, count each one, and add.

Worked example 1: Seats at the ends

Coursebook, 5.4, probability from equally likely permutations. New numbers.

Five friends, including Dev and Eva, sit in a row of five seats in a random order. Find the probability that Dev and Eva sit in the two end seats.

  1. Possible arrangements: \(5!=120\). B1
  2. Favourable: Dev and Eva at the ends in \(2\) ways, the other three in between in \(3!\) ways, so \(2\times3!=12\). M1
  3. \(\mathrm{P}=\dfrac{12}{120}=\dfrac{1}{10}\) A1

Answer: \(\dfrac{1}{10}\)

Worked example 2: A random selection

Coursebook, 5.4, probability from equally likely combinations. New numbers.

Three people are chosen at random from 5 boys and 4 girls. Find the probability that exactly two girls are chosen.

  1. Possible selections: \({}^{9}C_{3}=84\). B1
  2. Favourable: \({}^{4}C_{2}\times{}^{5}C_{1}=6\times5=30\). M1
  3. \(\mathrm{P}=\dfrac{30}{84}=\dfrac{5}{14}\) A1

Answer: \(\dfrac{5}{14}\)

Common mistakes

  • Counting the favourable outcomes as permutations but the possible outcomes as combinations, or the other way round.
  • For letters taken from a word with repeated letters, forgetting that identical letters give the same selection.
  • Adding probabilities of scenarios that overlap, so that some outcomes are counted twice.

(a) In how many ways can a team of 5 people be chosen from the group if exactly one adult is included? [2]

(b) In how many ways can a team of 5 people be chosen from the group if the team includes at least 2 boys and at least 1 girl? [4]

(c) How many different arrangements are there in which the 3 boys stand together and an adult is at each end of the line? [4]

Quick check 1

6 people, including Ali and Ben, sit in a row of 6 seats in a random order. Find the probability that Ali and Ben sit next to each other.

Worked solution
  1. Favourable: \(2\times5!=240\); possible: \(6!=720\).
  2. \(\dfrac{240}{720}=\dfrac{1}{3}\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{1}{3}\approx0.333\)

Quick check 2

4 people are chosen at random from a group of 5 men and 6 women. Find the probability that all 4 people chosen are women.

Worked solution
  1. Favourable: \({}^{6}C_{4}=15\); possible: \({}^{11}C_{4}=330\).
  2. \(\dfrac{15}{330}=\dfrac{1}{22}\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{1}{22}\approx0.0455\)

Quick check 3

3 people are chosen at random from a group of 6 men and 4 women. Find the probability that at least one woman is chosen.

Worked solution
  1. No woman: \(\dfrac{{}^{6}C_{3}}{{}^{10}C_{3}}=\dfrac{20}{120}=\dfrac{1}{6}\).
  2. \(1-\dfrac{1}{6}=\dfrac{5}{6}\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{5}{6}\approx0.833\)
