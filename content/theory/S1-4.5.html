4.5 Dependent events and conditional probability

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Probability & Statistics 1 Coursebook (Dean Chalmers), Chapter 4 Probability, printed pp.112–117 (Key points 4.9–4.10, Worked examples 4.12–4.16, Exercise 4F), together with the formal test for independence on pp.111–112 that continues Section 4.4. Own wording and new numbers.

Key words: dependent events, conditional probability, multiplication law, tree diagram, independent

Independence, now using conditional probability

Section 4.3 used the multiplication law \(\mathrm{P}(X\cap Y)=\mathrm{P}(X)\mathrm{P}(Y)\) as a definition of independence. A more formal statement, completed at the top of the pages for this section, is that \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent if and only if $$\mathrm{P}(X\mid Y)=\mathrm{P}(X\mid Y').$$ In words: the probability that \(X\) occurs is the same whether or not \(Y\) has occurred, so \(X\) is unaffected by \(Y\). When this holds, it is also true that \(\mathrm{P}(X\mid Y)=\mathrm{P}(X)\). Events can be independent without being mutually exclusive.

Worked example 1: Testing independence with conditionals

Book: pages 111–112 (Explore 4.3 style, new numbers)

A fair six-sided die is rolled. Let \(A\) be the event “the score is a multiple of 3” and \(B\) the event “the score is even”. Determine whether \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive, and whether they are independent.

  1. \(A=\{3,6\}\) and \(B=\{2,4,6\}\) share the outcome 6, so the events are not mutually exclusive. B1
  2. Given \(B\), the outcomes are 2, 4, 6, of which one is a multiple of 3, so \(\mathrm{P}(A\mid B)=\dfrac{1}{3}\). M1
  3. Given \(B'\), the outcomes are 1, 3, 5, of which one is a multiple of 3, so \(\mathrm{P}(A\mid B')=\dfrac{1}{3}\). A1
  4. \(\mathrm{P}(A\mid B)=\mathrm{P}(A\mid B')\), so \(A\) and \(B\) are independent. (Also \(\mathrm{P}(A)=\dfrac{2}{6}=\dfrac{1}{3}\).) A1

Answer: not mutually exclusive; independent

Dependent events and the general multiplication law

Two events are dependent when the occurrence of one affects the probability of the other. Selections without replacement are a typical case: the probability for the second selection depends on what was drawn first.

The multiplication law for independent events is a special case of the general multiplication law, which uses a conditional probability.

Key point. For any two events \(A\) and \(B\), $$\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)=\mathrm{P}(A)\,\mathrm{P}(B\mid A)=\mathrm{P}(B)\,\mathrm{P}(A\mid B).$$ These two products are identical. If \(A\) and \(B\) are independent then \(\mathrm{P}(B\mid A)=\mathrm{P}(B)\) and the law reduces to the product of the two ordinary probabilities.

Worked example 2: Selections without replacement

Book: Worked example 4.12, p.113; Exercise 4F Q1–3

Two children are selected at random from a group of 8 boys and 10 girls. Find the probability that the selection consists of two girls.

  1. The first child is selected from 18 and the second from the remaining 17, so the selections are dependent. B1
  2. \(\mathrm{P}(\text{two girls})=\mathrm{P}(G_1)\times\mathrm{P}(G_2\mid G_1)=\dfrac{10}{18}\times\dfrac{9}{17}=\dfrac{5}{17}\) M1 A1

Answer: \(\dfrac{5}{17}\approx0.294\)

Worked example 3: A tree diagram and a total probability

Book: Worked example 4.13, p.113; Exercise 4F Q4

A student travels by bus on 65% of school mornings and walks on the other mornings. The student is late on 8% of the mornings that they travel by bus, and late on 20% of the mornings that they walk. Find the probability that the student is late on a randomly chosen morning.

  1. From a tree diagram, \(\mathrm{P}(\text{bus and late})=0.65\times0.08=0.052\) and \(\mathrm{P}(\text{walk and late})=0.35\times0.20=0.070\). M1 A1
  2. \(\mathrm{P}(\text{late})=0.052+0.070=0.122\) A1

Answer: \(0.122\)

Rearranging to find a conditional probability

The same identity can be rearranged when \(\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)\) and \(\mathrm{P}(A)\) are known.

Key point. Provided \(\mathrm{P}(A)\neq0\) and \(\mathrm{P}(B)\neq0\), $$\mathrm{P}(B\mid A)=\frac{\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathrm{P}(A)},\qquad\mathrm{P}(A\mid B)=\frac{\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathrm{P}(B)}.$$ This is the formal formula for conditional probability. It agrees with the reduced-sample-space counts of Section 4.4, because dividing two probabilities that share the same denominator is the same as dividing the corresponding counts. On a tree diagram the same formula is used “backwards”: first find the probability of the given end, then divide the relevant combined probability by that total.

Worked example 4: Using the formal formula

Book: Worked example 4.14, p.114; Exercise 4F Q9

Given that \(\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)=0.28\) and \(\mathrm{P}(B)=0.7\), find \(\mathrm{P}(A\mid B)\).

  1. \(\mathrm{P}(A\mid B)=\dfrac{\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathrm{P}(B)}=\dfrac{0.28}{0.7}=0.4\) M1 A1

Answer: \(0.4\)

Worked example 5: A conditional probability from a tree

Book: Worked example 4.16, pp.114–115; Exercise 4F Q8, Q13–14

A messenger cycles on 40% of deliveries and walks on the other 60%. The messenger is delayed on 2% of the deliveries made by bicycle and on 5% of the deliveries made on foot. Given that a delivery is delayed, find the probability that the messenger cycled.

  1. \(\mathrm{P}(\text{cycle and delayed})=0.40\times0.02=0.008\) and \(\mathrm{P}(\text{walk and delayed})=0.60\times0.05=0.030\), so \(\mathrm{P}(\text{delayed})=0.038\). M1 A1
  2. \(\mathrm{P}(\text{cycled}\mid\text{delayed})=\dfrac{0.008}{0.038}=\dfrac{4}{19}\approx0.211\) M1 A1

Answer: \(\dfrac{4}{19}\approx0.211\)

Common mistakes

  • Using \(\mathrm{P}(A)\mathrm{P}(B)\) when the events are dependent (for example, after drawing without replacement).
  • Dividing by the wrong probability in the formal formula: the denominator is the probability of the given event.
  • Forgetting both orders when two different types are selected “in any order”.
  • Treating “independent” as if it meant “mutually exclusive”.

Quick checks

Quick check 1

Given that \(\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)=0.24\) and \(\mathrm{P}(B)=0.6\), find \(\mathrm{P}(A\mid B)\).

Worked solution
  1. \(\mathrm{P}(A\mid B)=\dfrac{0.24}{0.6}=0.4\)

Answer: \(0.4\)

Quick check 2

Two counters are selected at random, without replacement, from a bag containing 4 red counters and 6 blue counters. Find the probability that both counters are red.

Worked solution
  1. \(\dfrac{4}{10}\times\dfrac{3}{9}=\dfrac{2}{15}\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{2}{15}\approx0.133\)

Quick check 3

A shopper pays by card on 40% of visits and by cash on the other visits. The shopper is offered a receipt on 8% of card payments and on 3% of cash payments. Find the probability that the shopper is offered a receipt on a randomly chosen visit.

Worked solution
  1. \(0.40\times0.08+0.60\times0.03=0.032+0.018=0.05\)

Answer: \(0.05\)

Quick check 4

A student walks to college on 70% of mornings and cycles on the other mornings. The student is late on 10% of the mornings that they walk and on 2% of the mornings that they cycle. Given that the student is late, find the probability that the student walked.

Worked solution
  1. \(\mathrm{P}(\text{late})=0.70\times0.10+0.30\times0.02=0.070+0.006=0.076\)
  2. \(\mathrm{P}(\text{walked}\mid\text{late})=\dfrac{0.070}{0.076}=\dfrac{35}{38}\approx0.921\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{35}{38}\approx0.921\)

Marks: M = method, A = accurate answer (depends on M), B = independent mark. Marks on worked examples are illustrative, not official. Give exact fractions or answers correct to 3 significant figures. If events are dependent, use a conditional probability in the product; the denominator of \(\mathrm{P}(A\mid B)\) is \(\mathrm{P}(B)\).
