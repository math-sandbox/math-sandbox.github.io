5.1 The factorial function

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Probability & Statistics 1 Coursebook (Dean Chalmers), Chapter 5 Permutations and combinations, printed p.124. Own wording and new numbers, following the coursebook definition of \(n!\), the sequence of values found by division, and the result \(0!=1\).

Key words: factorial

The factorial function

Counting problems often need products such as \(5\times4\times3\times2\times1\). For a positive integer \(n\), the product of all the integers from \(n\) down to 1 is called \(n\) factorial and is written \(n!\):

\[n!=n\times(n-1)\times(n-2)\times\cdots\times3\times2\times1.\]

So \(3!=6\), \(4!=24\), \(5!=120\) and \(6!=720\). The values grow very quickly: \(10!=3\,628\,800\). Most calculators have a factorial key, marked \(n!\) or \(x!\).

Why \(0!=1\)

Each factorial is the next one divided by a whole number: \(3!=4!\div4\), \(2!=3!\div3\) and \(1!=2!\div2=1\). Continuing the pattern one more step gives \(0!=1!\div1=1\). The value \(0!=1\) is used in many of the formulae in this chapter.

Simplifying quotients of factorials

Because \(n!=n\times(n-1)!\), a larger factorial divided by a smaller one cancels down to a short product. For example, \(\dfrac{7!}{4!}=7\times6\times5=210\). In the same way a difference such as \(5!-4!\) can be simplified by taking out the common factor \(4!\): \(5!-4!=4!\,(5-1)=96\).

Worked example 1: A quotient of factorials

Coursebook, 5.1, cancelling factorials. New numbers.

Evaluate \(\dfrac{8!}{5!}\) without finding \(8!\) in full.

  1. \(\dfrac{8!}{5!}=\dfrac{8\times7\times6\times5!}{5!}\) M1
  2. \(=8\times7\times6=336\) A1

Answer: \(336\)

Worked example 2: An equation with factorials

Coursebook, 5.1, simplifying factorial expressions. New numbers.

Find the positive integer \(n\) for which \(\dfrac{(n+2)!}{n!}=56\).

  1. \(\dfrac{(n+2)!}{n!}=(n+2)(n+1)\), so \((n+2)(n+1)=56\). M1
  2. \(n^2+3n-54=0\), so \((n+9)(n-6)=0\) and the positive integer is \(n=6\). A1

Answer: \(n=6\)

Common mistakes

  • Writing \(0!=0\). By the pattern above, \(0!=1\).
  • Cancelling \(\dfrac{8!}{4!}\) as \(2!\). Factorials do not divide like ordinary numbers; \(\dfrac{8!}{4!}=8\times7\times6\times5\).
  • Treating \(2\times3!\) as \(6!\). In fact \(2\times3!=12\) and \(6!=720\).

Past paper

There is no whole past-paper question on this subsection alone.

Quick check 1

Evaluate \(\dfrac{9!}{7!}\).

Worked solution
  1. \(\dfrac{9!}{7!}=9\times8=72\)

Answer: \(72\)

Quick check 2

Find the positive integer \(n\) such that \(\dfrac{n!}{(n-2)!}=90\).

Worked solution
  1. \(\dfrac{n!}{(n-2)!}=n(n-1)\), so \(n(n-1)=90\).
  2. \(n^2-n-90=0\), so \((n-10)(n+9)=0\) and \(n=10\).

Answer: \(n=10\)

Quick check 3

Evaluate \(\dfrac{7!-6!}{5!}\).

Worked solution
  1. \(7!-6!=7\times6!-6!=6\times6!\)
  2. \(\dfrac{6\times6!}{5!}=6\times6=36\)

Answer: \(36\)
