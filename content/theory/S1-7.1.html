7.1 The binomial distribution

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Probability & Statistics 1 Coursebook (Dean Chalmers), Chapter 7 The binomial and geometric distributions, printed pp.166–174. Own wording and new numbers, following the coursebook conditions for a binomial model and the formula for binomial probabilities.

Key words: binomial distribution, trial, success, independent

When is a binomial model suitable?

A random variable \(X\) has a binomial distribution when it counts the number of successes in a fixed number of trials and:

  • there is a fixed number \(n\) of trials;
  • each trial has only two outcomes, success or failure;
  • the trials are independent;
  • the probability of success \(p\) is the same for every trial.

We write \(X\sim\mathrm{B}(n, p)\). The probability of failure is \(q=1-p\).

Binomial probabilities

Each particular arrangement of \(r\) successes and \(n-r\) failures has probability \(p^rq^{n-r}\), and there are \({}^nC_r\) such arrangements, so

\[P(X=r)={}^nC_r\,p^r\,q^{n-r},\qquad r=0, 1, 2, \dots, n.\]

For cumulative probabilities add terms, or use the complement. For example \(P(X\ge 1)=1-P(X=0)=1-q^n\).

Mean and variance

If \(X\sim\mathrm{B}(n, p)\), then \(E(X)=np\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=np(1-p)=npq\).

Worked example 1: At most two

Coursebook, 7.1, binomial probabilities. New numbers.

\(X\sim\mathrm{B}(8, 0.25)\). Find \(P(X\le 2)\).

  1. \(P(X=0)=0.75^8=0.10011\) M1
  2. \(P(X=1)=8\times0.25\times0.75^7=0.26697\) and \(P(X=2)=28\times0.25^2\times0.75^6=0.31146\) M1
  3. \(P(X\le 2)=0.10011+0.26697+0.31146=0.679\) A1

Answer: \(0.679\) (3 s.f.)

Worked example 2: Mean and variance

Coursebook, 7.1, expectation and variance. New numbers.

A fair die is rolled 30 times. \(X\) is the number of sixes. Find \(E(X)\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\).

  1. \(X\sim\mathrm{B}\bigl(30, \tfrac{1}{6}\bigr)\) B1
  2. \(E(X)=30\times\dfrac{1}{6}=5\) B1
  3. \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=5\times\dfrac{5}{6}=4.17\) (3 s.f.) B1

Answer: \(E(X)=5\), \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=\dfrac{25}{6}\)

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the factor \({}^nC_r\).
  • Treating \(P(X\lt 10)\) as including 10. Fewer than 10 means 0 to 9.
  • Using a binomial model when the trials are not independent, for example when objects are taken without replacement from a small group.

The number of books read by members of a book club each year has the binomial distribution \(\mathrm{B}(12, 0.7)\).

(i) State the greatest number of books that could be read by a member of the book club in a particular year and find the probability that a member reads this number of books. [2]

(ii) Find the probability that a member reads fewer than 10 books in a particular year. [3]

Mark scheme
  1. (i) Greatest number is 12. B1 Official MS: B1, max = 12 (implied by P(12) with power 12).
  2. (i) \(P(12)=0.7^{12}=0.0138\) B1 Official MS: B1, 0.0138; accept 0.014.
  3. (ii) \(P(X<10)=1-P(10, 11, 12)\) M1 Official MS: M1, binomial term 12Cr (0.7)^r (0.3)^(12−r) or 12Cr p^r q^(12−r) with p + q strictly between 0.99 and 1.00.
  4. (ii) \(=1-{}^{12}C_{10}(0.7)^{10}(0.3)^2-12(0.7)^{11}(0.3)-(0.7)^{12}=1-0.2528\) A1 Official MS: A1, correct unsimplified expression OE.
  5. (ii) \(=0.747\) A1 Official MS: A1, correct answer.

Quick check 1

\(X\sim\mathrm{B}(6, 0.3)\). Find \(P(X=2)\).

Worked solution
  1. \(P(X=2)={}^6C_2\times0.3^2\times0.7^4=15\times0.09\times0.2401\)

Answer: \(0.324\) (3 s.f.)

Quick check 2

\(X\sim\mathrm{B}(5, 0.2)\). Find \(P(X\ge 1)\).

Worked solution
  1. \(P(X=0)=0.8^5=0.32768\)
  2. \(P(X\ge 1)=1-0.32768\)

Answer: \(0.672\) (3 s.f.)
