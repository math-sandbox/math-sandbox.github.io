4.3 Independent events and the multiplication law

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Probability & Statistics 1 Coursebook (Dean Chalmers), Chapter 4 Probability, pp.100–107 (Key point 4.8, Worked examples 4.5–4.8, Exercises 4C and 4D). Own wording and new numbers.

Key words: independent, multiplication law, tree diagram, with replacement

Independent events

Two events are independent if either can occur without being affected by the occurrence of the other. Selections with replacement, and separate actions such as rolling two dice, give independent events. (Selections without replacement are not independent, and are studied later.)

For independent events the multiplication law holds:

$$\mathrm{P}(A\text{ and }B)=\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)=\mathrm{P}(A)\times\mathrm{P}(B).$$

The same law extends to any number of independent events: \(\mathrm{P}(A\cap B\cap C\cap\ldots)=\mathrm{P}(A)\times\mathrm{P}(B)\times\mathrm{P}(C)\times\cdots\).

The multiplication law can also be used in reverse, as a test: if \(\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)=\mathrm{P}(A)\times\mathrm{P}(B)\), then \(A\) and \(B\) are independent, and if the two sides are not equal then the events are not independent.

Tree diagrams

A tree diagram shows the outcomes of successive selections, with a probability on each branch. For independent events, multiply from left to right along a path. Mutually exclusive paths are then added vertically. The set of all complete paths is exhaustive, so their probabilities sum to 1.

As an alternative to a tree, a possibility diagram (or list of equally likely outcomes) can be used when every combined outcome is equally likely.

Worked example 1: A tree diagram, with replacement

Book: Key point 4.8 and the tree on pp.100–101; Exercise 4C Q1–2

A bag contains 3 red balls and 5 green balls. One ball is selected at random, replaced, and then a second ball is selected. Find the probability that the two balls are (a) both red (b) different colours.

  1. (a) \(\mathrm{P}(RR)=\dfrac38\times\dfrac38=\dfrac{9}{64}\) M1 A1
  2. (b) \(\mathrm{P}(RG\text{ or }GR)=\dfrac38\times\dfrac58+\dfrac58\times\dfrac38=\dfrac{15}{64}+\dfrac{15}{64}=\dfrac{30}{64}=\dfrac{15}{32}\) M1 A1

Answer: (a) \(\dfrac{9}{64}=0.141\) (3 s.f.) (b) \(\dfrac{15}{32}=0.469\) (3 s.f.)

Worked example 2: Independent repeated trials

Book: Worked example 4.6, p.102; Exercise 4C Q4–6

Each morning the probability that the school bus is late is \(0.3\), independently of other mornings. Find the probability that, on two mornings, the bus is late on (a) the second morning only (b) exactly one of the two mornings (c) at least one of the two mornings.

  1. (a) \(\mathrm{P}(NL)=0.7\times0.3=0.21\) B1
  2. (b) \(\mathrm{P}(LN\text{ or }NL)=0.3\times0.7+0.7\times0.3=0.42\) M1 A1
  3. (c) \(1-\mathrm{P}(NN)=1-0.7^2=1-0.49=0.51\) M1 A1

Answer: (a) \(0.21\) (b) \(0.42\) (c) \(0.51\)

Worked example 3: Testing for independence

Book: Worked examples 4.7–4.8, pp.105–106; Exercise 4D

In a group of 80 students, 40 are male and 30 study music. Of the males, 15 study music. One student is selected at random. Show that the events “the student is male” and “the student studies music” are independent.

  1. \(\mathrm{P}(M)=\dfrac{40}{80}=\dfrac12\), \(\mathrm{P}(S)=\dfrac{30}{80}=\dfrac{3}{8}\), \(\mathrm{P}(M\cap S)=\dfrac{15}{80}=\dfrac{3}{16}\) B1
  2. \(\mathrm{P}(M)\times\mathrm{P}(S)=\dfrac12\times\dfrac38=\dfrac{3}{16}=\mathrm{P}(M\cap S)\) M1 A1
  3. The multiplication law holds, so the events are independent A1

Answer: independent, because \(\mathrm{P}(M\cap S)=\mathrm{P}(M)\mathrm{P}(S)=\dfrac{3}{16}\)

Quick checks

Quick check 1

Events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent, with \(\mathrm{P}(A)=0.4\) and \(\mathrm{P}(B)=0.5\). Find \(\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)\).

Worked solution
  1. \(\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)=0.4\times0.5=0.2\)

Answer: \(0.2\)

Quick check 2

The probability that a train is late on any morning is \(0.2\), independently of other mornings. Find the probability that the train is late on exactly one of two mornings.

Worked solution
  1. \(\mathrm{P}(LN)+\mathrm{P}(NL)=0.2\times0.8+0.8\times0.2=0.32\)

Answer: \(0.32\)

Quick check 3

Events \(A\) and \(B\) are such that \(\mathrm{P}(A)=0.5\), \(\mathrm{P}(B)=0.4\) and \(\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)=0.2\). Find \(\mathrm{P}(A)\times\mathrm{P}(B)\), and hence decide whether \(A\) and \(B\) are independent.

Worked solution
  1. \(\mathrm{P}(A)\times\mathrm{P}(B)=0.5\times0.4=0.2\)
  2. This equals \(\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)\), so the events are independent

Answer: \(0.2\); yes, they are independent

Quick check 4

A fair coin is tossed twice. Find the probability of obtaining two heads.

Worked solution
  1. The tosses are independent: \(\dfrac12\times\dfrac12=\dfrac14\)

Answer: \(\dfrac14=0.25\)

Marks: M = method, A = accurate answer (depends on M), B = independent mark. Marks on worked examples are illustrative, not official. Give exact fractions or answers correct to 3 significant figures. Independent is not the same as mutually exclusive: mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities cannot be independent, because then \(\mathrm{P}(A\cap B)=0\) but \(\mathrm{P}(A)\mathrm{P}(B)\gt 0\).
