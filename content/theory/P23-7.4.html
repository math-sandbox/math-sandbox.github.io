7.4 Binomial expansion of \((a+x)^n\) for values of \(n\) that are not positive integers

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Pure Mathematics 2 and 3 Coursebook (Sue Pemberton, Julian Gilbey), Chapter 7, printed pp.177–178. Own wording and new numbers, following the coursebook method of taking out a factor \(a^n\) so that the bracket begins with 1, and the resulting range of validity.

Key words: binomial expansion

Taking out a factor

The binomial expansion for a value of \(n\) that is not a positive integer applies to a bracket of the form \((1+y)^n\). For \((a+x)^n\), first take out the factor \(a^n\):

\[(a+x)^n=a^n\left(1+\dfrac{x}{a}\right)^n=a^n\left(1+n\left(\dfrac{x}{a}\right)+\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2!}\left(\dfrac{x}{a}\right)^2+\cdots\right).\]

Remember that the whole bracket is raised to the power, so the factor taken out is \(a^n\), not \(a\). For example, \((4+x)^{1/2}=4^{1/2}\left(1+\dfrac{x}{4}\right)^{1/2}=2\left(1+\dfrac{x}{4}\right)^{1/2}\).

Range of validity

The expansion of \(\left(1+\dfrac{x}{a}\right)^n\) is valid for \(\left\lvert\dfrac{x}{a}\right\rvert<1\), that is \(\lvert x\rvert<\lvert a\rvert\). More generally, the expansion of \((a+bx)^n\) is valid for \(\lvert x\rvert<\left\lvert\dfrac{a}{b}\right\rvert\).

Worked example 1: A square root

Coursebook, 7.4, expanding \((a+x)^n\) for a fractional \(n\). New numbers.

Expand \(\sqrt{4+x}\) in ascending powers of \(x\) up to and including the term in \(x^2\), and state the set of values of \(x\) for which the expansion is valid.

  1. \(\sqrt{4+x}=2\left(1+\dfrac{x}{4}\right)^{1/2}=2\left(1+\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac{x}{4}+\dfrac{\frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)}{2}\left(\dfrac{x}{4}\right)^2+\cdots\right)\) M1
  2. \(=2+\dfrac{x}{4}-\dfrac{x^2}{64}\) A1
  3. Valid for \(\lvert x\rvert<4\). B1

Answer: \(2+\dfrac{1}{4}x-\dfrac{1}{64}x^2\), valid for \(\lvert x\rvert<4\)

Worked example 2: A negative power

Coursebook, 7.4, expanding \((a+bx)^n\) for a negative integer \(n\). New numbers.

Expand \((2-x)^{-2}\) in ascending powers of \(x\) up to and including the term in \(x^2\).

  1. \((2-x)^{-2}=\dfrac{1}{4}\left(1-\dfrac{x}{2}\right)^{-2}=\dfrac{1}{4}\left(1+(-2)\left(-\dfrac{x}{2}\right)+\dfrac{(-2)(-3)}{2}\left(-\dfrac{x}{2}\right)^2+\cdots\right)\) M1
  2. \(=\dfrac{1}{4}\left(1+x+\dfrac{3}{4}x^2\right)=\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{1}{4}x+\dfrac{3}{16}x^2\) A1

Answer: \(\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{1}{4}x+\dfrac{3}{16}x^2\)

Common mistakes

  • Taking out \(a\) instead of \(a^n\), for example writing \((9+x)^{1/2}=9\left(1+\dfrac{x}{9}\right)^{1/2}\) instead of \(3\left(1+\dfrac{x}{9}\right)^{1/2}\).
  • Forgetting to divide the \(x\) term inside the bracket by \(a\).
  • Stating the range of validity as \(\lvert x\rvert<1\) for \((a+bx)^n\), instead of \(\lvert x\rvert<\left\lvert\dfrac{a}{b}\right\rvert\).

Quick check 1

The expansion of \((6+2x)^{-2}\) in ascending powers of \(x\) is valid for \(\lvert x\rvert<k\). Find the value of \(k\).

Worked solution
  1. \((6+2x)^{-2}=6^{-2}\left(1+\dfrac{x}{3}\right)^{-2}\), valid for \(\left\lvert\dfrac{x}{3}\right\rvert<1\).

Answer: \(k=3\)

Quick check 2

Find the coefficient of \(x\) in the expansion of \(\sqrt{9+4x}\) in ascending powers of \(x\).

Worked solution
  1. \(\sqrt{9+4x}=3\left(1+\dfrac{4x}{9}\right)^{1/2}=3\left(1+\dfrac{2x}{9}+\cdots\right)\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{2}{3}\approx0.667\)

Quick check 3

Find the coefficient of \(x^2\) in the expansion of \(\dfrac{1}{3+2x}\) in ascending powers of \(x\).

Worked solution
  1. \((3+2x)^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{3}\left(1-\dfrac{2x}{3}+\dfrac{4x^2}{9}-\cdots\right)\)

Answer: \(\dfrac{4}{27}\approx0.148\)
