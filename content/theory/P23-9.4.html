9.4 The vector equation of a line

Based on Cambridge International AS & A Level Mathematics: Pure Mathematics 2 & 3 Coursebook (Sue Pemberton, series editor Julian Gilbey), Chapter 9 Vectors, printed pp.231–235. Own wording and new numbers, following the coursebook form r = a + λb and its use for points on a line and perpendicular distances.

Key words: vector equation of a line, direction vector, foot of the perpendicular

The equation \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}+\lambda\mathbf{b}\)

A line through the point with position vector \(\mathbf{a}\), in the direction of the vector \(\mathbf{b}\), has vector equation

\[\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}+\lambda\mathbf{b},\]

where \(\lambda\) is a scalar parameter. Each value of \(\lambda\) gives the position vector of one point on the line. \(\mathbf{b}\) is called the direction vector.

The line through \(A\) and \(B\) has equation \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}+\lambda(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a})\). The equation of a line is not unique: any point on the line and any multiple of the direction vector can be used.

Points on a line and the foot of a perpendicular

Write a general point \(P\) on the line in component form, for example \(P=(1+2\lambda,\;\lambda,\;2-3\lambda)\). To check whether a point lies on the line, find \(\lambda\) from one component and test the others.

To find the foot \(N\) of the perpendicular from a point \(A\) to the line, use \(\overrightarrow{AN}\cdot\mathbf{b}=0\) to find \(\lambda\). The perpendicular distance is then \(AN\).

Worked example 1: Does a point lie on a line?

Coursebook, 9.4, points on a line. New numbers.

Line \(l\): \(\mathbf{r}=2\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}+\lambda(\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}-2\mathbf{k})\). Does \((5, 8, -6)\) lie on \(l\)?

  1. \(x\): \(2+\lambda=5\), so \(\lambda=3\) M1
  2. Then \(y=-1+9=8\) and \(z=-6\), both correct M1
  3. So the point lies on \(l\). A1

Answer: Yes, with \(\lambda=3\)

Worked example 2: Perpendicular distance

Coursebook, 9.4, foot of the perpendicular. New numbers.

Find the perpendicular distance from \(A(3, 4, 0)\) to the line \(\mathbf{r}=\lambda(\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k})\).

  1. \(\overrightarrow{AN}=(\lambda-3)\mathbf{i}+(\lambda-4)\mathbf{j}+\lambda\mathbf{k}\) B1
  2. \((\lambda-3)+(\lambda-4)+\lambda=0\), so \(\lambda=\dfrac{7}{3}\) M1 A1
  3. \(\overrightarrow{AN}=-\dfrac{2}{3}\mathbf{i}-\dfrac{5}{3}\mathbf{j}+\dfrac{7}{3}\mathbf{k}\), so \(AN=\dfrac{\sqrt{78}}{3}=2.94\) A1

Answer: \(2.94\) (3 s.f.)

Common mistakes

  • Using the position vector of a point as the direction vector.
  • Using \(\overrightarrow{ON}\cdot\mathbf{b}=0\) instead of \(\overrightarrow{AN}\cdot\mathbf{b}=0\).
  • Finding \(\lambda\) from one component and forgetting to check the other two.

Quick check 1

\(l\): \(\mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\2\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-3\end{pmatrix}\). The point \(P\) on \(l\) has \(x=7\). Find the \(z\)-coordinate of \(P\).

Worked solution
  1. \(1+2\lambda=7\), so \(\lambda=3\)
  2. \(z=2+3\times(-3)\)

Answer: \(-7\)

Quick check 2

\(l\): \(\mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\-4\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\\2\end{pmatrix}\). Find the \(x\)-coordinate where \(l\) meets the plane \(z=0\).

Worked solution
  1. \(-4+2\lambda=0\), so \(\lambda=2\)
  2. \(x=2+6\)

Answer: \(8\)

Quick check 3

\(l\): \(\mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\) and \(A\) is \((1, 5, 6)\). Find \(\lambda\) at the foot of the perpendicular from \(A\).

Worked solution
  1. \(\overrightarrow{AN}=(3+\lambda)\mathbf{i}-5\mathbf{j}-5\mathbf{k}\)
  2. \(\overrightarrow{AN}\cdot\mathbf{i}=3+\lambda=0\)

Answer: \(\lambda=-3\)
